L1 · Foundations of Leadership and Motivation I

What is leadership and why does it matter? · LEM: general overview

Leadership is influence, not position

Many people associate leadership with authority, hierarchy, charisma or a job title. The course rejects that view: leadership exists whenever someone influences other people's behaviour, attitudes, decisions, motivation and commitment towards a common goal. It often happens before people hold a formal leadership position. A junior analyst who gets the team to adopt a better process is leading; a director who only signs approvals may not be.

Assigned leadership

Based on a formal position (team lead, director). Authority is given by the organisation.

Emergent leadership

Based on how others respond: people come to see someone as a leader through their behaviour, regardless of title (Northouse, 2022).

Influence and the five bases of power (French & Raven, 1959)

Power is the capacity to influence; influence is power in use. Power itself is neutral: its consequences depend on how it is used (L3).

BaseSourceTypeExampleTypical effect

Personal power (expert, referent) tends to produce commitment; position power (legitimate, reward, coercive) tends to produce compliance. Effective leaders combine them and rely mostly on personal power.

Leadership vs. management (Kotter, 1990)

Kotter argues they are complementary, not opposed: management copes with complexity, leadership copes with change. Organisations need both.

FunctionManagement (complexity)Leadership (change)
Deciding what to doPlanning and budgeting: targets, steps, resourcesSetting a direction: vision and strategies
Creating networks of peopleOrganising and staffing: structure, jobs, delegationAligning people: communicating the direction so people understand and believe it
Ensuring it gets doneControlling and problem-solving: monitor against planMotivating and inspiring: appealing to needs, values, emotions
ResultOrder, consistency, predictabilityMovement, adaptation, commitment
Example

Launching a new product line: management builds the Gantt chart, budget and staffing; leadership explains why the launch matters, gets sales and engineering to believe in it, and keeps people going when the first version fails.

Leadership effectiveness

Effectiveness is the positive and sustainable impact leadership creates on individuals, teams, organisations and outcomes (Gomes, 2020). Kaiser, Hogan and Craig (2008) stress that effectiveness should be judged by the performance of the team or organisation the leader is responsible for, not only by how much the leader is liked or promoted, and that failure rates among managers are high: leadership matters for the fate of organisations.

Leadership and motivation

Motivation drives effort, persistence, engagement and commitment. Leaders cannot inject motivation; they shape the conditions under which people work and develop. Self-Determination Theory (Deci, Olafsen & Ryan, 2017) says motivation depends on three basic needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness (developed in L8).

Motivation is both a consequence and a condition of leadership

Consequence: good leadership produces motivated people. Condition: motivated followers make leadership easier (psychological favourability in the LEM). It is a loop.

Key messages L1
  1. Leadership is influence, not position.
  2. Technical excellence does not automatically make an effective leader.
  3. Leadership exists to mobilise (motivate) people towards shared goals.
  4. Efficacy depends on the interaction of cycles, styles and the characteristics of leader, followers and context.
  5. Motivation is both a consequence and a condition of leadership.
  6. Leadership is a skill that can be developed, not something you have or lack.
For your plan

The "My leadership story" exercise (leaders you admire) is raw material for your philosophy: what did those leaders believe, do and measure? Which of their sources of power do you want to rely on?

L2 · Foundations of Leadership II: the theories

How has leadership research tried to explain effectiveness? · LEM: overview + leadership cycles

Models are simplified representations of a complex reality: they help to understand, diagnose, predict and improve. No theory explains everything; each one answers a different question. The LEM integrates them.

Leadership emergence vs. leadership effectiveness

Emergence

The extent to which a person is perceived as a leader by others. Who gets chosen, noticed, followed.

Effectiveness

The extent to which leadership produces positive and sustainable outcomes for people, teams and results (Kaiser et al., 2008).

Example

The most talkative, confident student is usually chosen to lead the group project (emergence). Whether the project is good and the group stays united is a different question (effectiveness). Extraversion predicts emergence more than effectiveness.

Trait approach

Assumption: some stable personal characteristics make people more likely to become leaders. Classic traits: intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, sociability (Northouse, 2022). Modern research uses the Big Five.

In the LEM, traits survive as leader characteristics within the antecedent factors.

Behavioural approach

Assumption: effectiveness depends on what leaders do. Because behaviours can be trained, leadership can be learned. This is the foundation of leadership development, Pro●Leader included.

Task-oriented (initiating structure)

Organising work, defining roles, setting goals and standards, scheduling. Helps people achieve objectives.

Relationship-oriented (consideration)

Respect, trust, attention to wellbeing, making people comfortable. Focuses on people.

Ohio State and Michigan studies, and Blake and Mouton's Managerial Grid (high task + high people = "team management"), all conclude that effective leaders balance both. DeRue et al. (2011) found that behaviours explain more variance in effectiveness than traits, and that behaviours partly mediate the effect of traits: traits → behaviours → effectiveness. Together they explain at least 31% of the variance.

Limitation: no universal "best behaviour" was found; the same behaviour works in one situation and fails in another. That opened the door to contingency theories.

Full Range Leadership Model (Bass & Riggio, 2006)

Leadership behaviours range from most passive and least effective to most active and effective:

Transactional leadership regulates performance through exchanges: contingent reward (reward for achieved goals), active MBE and passive MBE. Evidence: transformational .44, contingent reward .39, laissez-faire −.37; MBE is inconsistent (Judge & Piccolo, 2004). Transformational and contingent reward are strongly correlated (.80): effective leaders use both.

Key messages L2
  1. Every theory explains something; none explains everything.
  2. Traits explain who emerges, not who becomes effective.
  3. Behavioural approaches moved attention from who leaders are to what they do, the basis of leadership development.
  4. Effectiveness depends partly on fit between behaviours, followers and situation.
  5. Effective leaders rarely rely on a single style.
  6. Theories should guide judgement, not replace it.

L3 · Ethical leadership and diversity

Can leadership be considered effective if it lacks ethical legitimacy? · LEM: leadership cycles

No leadership is ethically neutral

Because leadership is influence, it always affects other people's opportunities, wellbeing, development and decisions. That creates ethical responsibility. Ethical leadership is the responsible exercise of influence through behaviour consistent with integrity, fairness, honesty and respect. Ethical leaders behave ethically and promote ethical conduct in others: they act as a "moral person" and a "moral manager" (Brown & Treviño, 2006).

Leadership legitimacy

The perception that a leader's influence, decisions and actions are appropriate, fair and acceptable. People trust and follow leaders they see as fair and responsible, so ethics feeds legitimacy and legitimacy feeds influence.

Example: "ethics versus success"

Morais, Abrams and Randsley de Moura (2020) show that people are more willing to accept unethical behaviour from leaders who bring success. The danger: results can "buy" tolerance for misconduct in the short term, while trust and sustainability erode. Think of sales leaders who hit targets with aggressive mis-selling until a scandal breaks.

Power

Power is the capacity to influence people, decisions, resources and outcomes. It is neither ethical nor unethical in itself. The lecture's message: power can improve or deteriorate judgement. It gives the ability to act, but it can also reduce perspective-taking and make leaders less attentive to others' views.

Ethical dilemmas: right vs. right

A dilemma occurs when two or more legitimate values point to different actions. It is not "right vs. wrong" but "right vs. right" (Ciulla, 2020).

TensionExample
Loyalty vs. fairnessPromote a loyal long-time team member or the stronger external candidate?
Individual vs. collectiveGrant one person's flexible-hours request when it increases others' workload?
Truth vs. careTell the team now about possible layoffs, or wait until it is certain?
Short term vs. long termMeet the quarter's target or invest in training that will pay off next year?

Ethical leadership requires judgement, not just good intentions: making the competing values explicit, considering who is affected, and explaining the reasoning.

Cognitive biases (Kahneman, 2011)

Leaders decide with incomplete information and rely on heuristics (mental shortcuts). Shortcuts are useful but produce systematic errors: biases. Good intentions do not protect you from them.

Diversity vs. inclusion

Diversity: who is present

Differences among people: demographic, experiential, professional, cognitive. Gives access to more perspectives, information and problem-solving approaches.

Inclusion: who participates

The extent to which people are respected, heard, able to participate and to influence decisions (Shore et al., 2018). Combines belonging with being valued for one's uniqueness.

"Diversity without inclusion generates little value": a team can look diverse while only the same two people decide.

Psychological safety (Edmondson)

A shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking: people can ask questions, admit mistakes, raise concerns and challenge assumptions without fear of humiliation, punishment or rejection. It does not mean lower standards (see the 2×2 in L4).

Ethics through the LEM: congruence

Ethical leadership in the LEM means congruence between what you believe (philosophy), what you do (practice) and how you evaluate success (criteria). A leader who preaches "people first" but evaluates only revenue is incongruent, and people notice. Incongruence destroys trust and legitimacy.

Key messages L3
  1. There is no ethically neutral leadership.
  2. Ethical leadership requires judgement, not merely good intentions.
  3. Power can improve or deteriorate judgement.
  4. Information processing is vulnerable to biases.
  5. Diversity without inclusion generates little value.
  6. Psychological safety does not mean lower standards.
  7. Sustainable effectiveness requires ethical legitimacy.
For your plan

State an explicit ethical commitment in your philosophy, and include at least one human criterion (fairness, trust, voice), not only task criteria. Name the bias you are most prone to and how your practice protects against it.

L4 · Team development: from groups to high-performing teams

How do leaders turn a group of people into a high-performing team? · LEM: antecedent factors (followers and context)

Four guiding questions of the lecture: who belonged, who supported whom, who could disagree or fail without cost, and who thought differently and what happened to them? The lecture closed with the individual 12-week plan for a newly merged team: the first draft of the final project.

Group vs. team

Group
  • Members answer for their own work
  • Individual goals
  • Result = sum of individual contributions
  • Individual accountability
Team (Katzenbach & Smith, 1993)

"A small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common purpose, set of performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable."

Interdependence + shared goals + mutual accountability.

Stages of team development (Tuckman, 1965; Tuckman & Jensen, 1977)

Not a law

The model came from small, mostly therapy and laboratory groups: it is a shared language, not a law. Stages are not linear: a new member, a new goal or a crisis can send a team back to storming.

Groupthink (Janis, 1972)

The wish for agreement overrides a realistic appraisal of alternatives.

More likely with
  • High cohesion
  • Isolation from outside views
  • A very directive leader
  • Time pressure
  • No method for deciding
Warning signs
  • Illusion of invulnerability
  • Illusion of unanimity
  • Self-censorship of doubts
  • Pressure on dissenters
  • "Mindguards" shielding the leader from bad news
Antidotes
  • Leader speaks last
  • Rotating devil's advocate
  • Pre-mortem: "a year from now we failed. Why?"
  • Silent input before discussion
  • Outside challenge; second-chance meeting

Empirical support for the full model is mixed: use it as a lens.

Diversity in teams (van Knippenberg et al., 2004)

Gains
  • More perspectives and information
  • Better analysis of complex problems
  • Natural brake on groupthink
  • Members prepare more because they expect disagreement (Phillips et al., 2009)
Costs
  • More coordination effort
  • More conflict early on
  • Faultlines: subgroups forming around shared attributes (Lau & Murnighan, 1998), e.g. "the ex-Company A people" vs "the ex-Company B people"
  • Tokenism: present but not heard

More diversity usually means more storming. That is the price of the gain, not a sign of failure. Diversity pays off only with inclusion, psychological safety and a good decision process.

Psychological safety × accountability (Edmondson, 2012, 2018)

What high-performing teams have in common

Project Aristotle (Google; Duhigg, 2016)

180+ teams: how members worked together mattered more than who was on the team. Five dynamics:

  1. Psychological safety (by far the most important)
  2. Dependability
  3. Structure and clarity
  4. Meaning
  5. Impact

Internal, correlational study: consistent with research, not proof of cause.

Hackman (2002): conditions for team effectiveness

Leaders cannot make a team perform, but they can create conditions:

  1. A real team (clear membership, interdependence, stability)
  2. A compelling direction
  3. An enabling structure (task design, composition, norms)
  4. A supportive organisational context
  5. Expert coaching at the right moments

Critical reading

We only see teams that won (survivorship bias); we rarely see the cost (who left, burned out, was never heard); and their context (selection, resources, luck) is not available to everyone.

Key messages L4
  1. Effective teams are built, not spontaneous.
  2. A group becomes a team through interdependence, shared goals and mutual accountability.
  3. Development is not linear.
  4. Trust comes before commitment; productive conflict is indispensable.
  5. Cohesion without challenge leads to groupthink; diversity without inclusion generates conflict without value.
  6. Psychological safety and high standards work together.
  7. Leaders create conditions more than they control results.
