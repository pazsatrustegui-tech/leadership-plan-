L5 · Communication, feedback and conflict management

Can leaders manage conflict in ways that support both performance and the quality of relationships? · LEM: antecedent factors + leadership styles

The nature and sources of conflict

Conflict arises when people perceive that their interests, goals, values or expectations are incompatible. It is inevitable and does not mean the leader failed. Its consequences depend on its nature, context and management.

Task conflict (about the work)

Disagreement about ideas, methods, priorities. At moderate levels it can improve decisions: it surfaces concerns and alternatives.

Relationship conflict (about the people)

Personal attacks, tension, resentment. Almost always damages cooperation and performance (Robbins & Judge). Process conflict (who does what) sits in between.

SourceConcernsExample
InterestsWhat people need or wantTwo managers both want the only available developer.
ValuesWhat people consider important or acceptable"Quality first" vs "ship fast and fix later".
PerceptionsHow people interpret events and behaviourOne reads a short email as rude; the sender meant efficient.
StructuralUnclear roles, competing priorities, limited resources, unmet expectationsSales promises dates that operations was never asked about.

Identifying the source lets the leader address the underlying issue rather than only its visible expression.

Communication Efficacy Model (Gomes, 2023)

Communication is the transmission of information, thoughts, opinions and ideas between two or more people, verbally or non-verbally. The model links:

Communication profiles

Characteristic patterns of behaviour (not fixed personality types) in how people express ideas, guide action and relate to others. They come in mirrored pairs:

FocusPositive profileNegative profile
Vision / futureOptimistic idealist: communicates a shared positive vision; expresses disagreement assertively. "This is hard, but here's why we can do it. I disagree with the timing, and here's my reason."Pessimistic idealist: communicates fear and pessimism; discourages initiative and innovation. "These projects always fail here."
Instruction / correctionPositive technical: constructive instructions focused on what can be done. "Start with the summary, then the data."Negative technical: instructions focused on what others should not do; restricts initiative. "Don't send it like that. Don't use that template."
RelationshipEncourager: promotes understanding, recognition, support and mutual trust.Discourager: disregards or punishes others' ideas, progress or performance.
Profiles vs. conflict styles

Conflict management styles describe how you approach a disagreement. Communication profiles describe how you communicate, inside and outside disagreements. A leader can collaborate (style) while speaking as a discourager (profile).

Mapping profiles to the LEM

Optimistic idealist ≈ vision + inspiration · Positive technical ≈ instruction · Encourager ≈ support + positive feedback · the negative profiles ≈ the suboptimal profile.

Conflict management styles (Thomas–Kilmann)

Styles differ in assertiveness (pursuing your own concerns) and cooperativeness (attending to the other's concerns).

Active listening, empathy, validate and redirect

Active listening

Deliberately attending to the message and checking it was understood: open questions, paraphrasing, clarifying, summarising. It does not require agreement. Seek first to understand, then to be understood.

Empathy

Seeking to understand the other's perspective and emotional experience. It helps identify obstacles and sustain cooperation while keeping expectations and accountability.

Validate + redirect

Validation acknowledges the concern; redirection moves to clarification, expectations, options or a next step. Validation is not agreement.

Validate → redirect, in practice

Employee: "This is the third time the requirements change. I'm fed up."
Validate: "I can see why it feels frustrating, especially after all the work you already put in."
Redirect: "Let's clarify exactly what changed, what can be reused, and agree a realistic next step. I'll also raise the change process with the client lead."

Workshop links

W5 Communication & accountability: sender → code → channel → receiver; the poorer the channel (email = words only), the clearer the code must be. "I sent it" ≠ "they got it": close the loop (ask, owner, deadline → read-back → follow-up).
W6 Five levels of listening (Covey): ignoring → pretending → selective → attentive/active → empathic. Every level can be a conscious choice; the real risk is the gap between the level you think you're at and the one you're actually at.

Key messages L5
  1. The problem is not conflict itself but how it is managed.
  2. Listening is a leadership skill.
  3. Validation does not mean agreement.
  4. Communication shapes the quality of relationships.
  5. Empathy can be a tool for improving performance.
For your plan

Say how you will handle disagreement (which style by default, when you will switch) and how you will evaluate a conflict as "well managed": practical outcome + relationship quality + alignment with your philosophy.

L6 · Leadership criteria: zooming out and sound judgement

How can zooming out help leaders define meaningful criteria, in line with their philosophy, followers and context? · LEM: leadership cycles (criteria) + antecedents + styles

Sound judgement

The ability to interpret situations carefully and make reasoned decisions with the available evidence. It involves separating observations from assumptions, considering alternative explanations and recognising uncertainty. It can be developed through reflection, feedback and reviewing decisions and their consequences.

Technical vs. adaptive challenges (Heifetz & Laurie, 1997)

Technical challengeAdaptive challenge
NatureClear problem, known solutionRequires people to learn and change assumptions, priorities, behaviours or relationships
Who solves itExperts, procedures, authorityThe people with the problem; the leader mobilises them
ExampleThe new software crashes on export: IT patches it.Teams won't share client information because the bonus system rewards individual sales.
Classic mistakeTreating an adaptive challenge as technical: sending everyone on a "collaboration training" when the incentive system punishes collaboration. Expertise alone cannot produce the learning adaptive change needs. Many situations mix both.

Zooming out: the balcony and the dance floor

Zooming out means stepping back from immediate demands to see relationships, recurring patterns and longer-term implications, then returning with a better-informed response. Heifetz's metaphor: on the dance floor you only see your partner; from the balcony you see the patterns of the whole room. Leaders need both: closeness to people's experience and enough distance to question first interpretations. Reflection, mentoring and decision reviews support this movement.

Zoom in

The individual and the moment: what is this person doing, feeling, needing right now? Also zooming in on yourself (self-awareness).

Zoom out

The system: roles, incentives, workflows, culture, history. If several people hit the same obstacle, the problem is probably the system.

Workshop link

W2–W3 "Zoom in / Zoom out": zoom in on yourself (self-awareness, the Leadership Orientations questionnaire), then zoom out to the system. The "planning vs on-sight navigation" staircase (plan ahead → observe reality → trial and error → practice → process → discipline → improvement) shows that leadership improves through cycles of planning and correcting against reality, which is the LEM feedback loop.

Leadership evaluation criteria

Criteria are explicit indicators used to assess whether, or to what extent, the intended leadership practices are implemented, and with what consequences. Good criteria are:

QualityMeaningWeakStrong
AlignedFollows from your philosophy and practicePhilosophy "people first"; criterion "revenue +10%" only"Each team member has a development goal agreed in a 1:1 by week 4"
ClearUnambiguous meaning"Better communication""Every decision from the Monday meeting is written down with an owner and a date"
SpecificSays exactly what is assessed"Team is motivated""Members rate 'I can influence how I do my work' (autonomy item)"
MeasurableAssessable with evidence (quantitative or qualitative)"People trust me""Average score ≥ 4/5 on 'I can raise problems with my manager without fear' in a monthly pulse survey"

Criteria should also be meaningful to the people affected, have an identified evidence source (survey, log, dashboard, observation, 1:1 notes) and a review time (weekly, monthly, end of quarter).

Two kinds of evidence (L6–L7)
  • Implementation evidence: did the intended action happen? (1:1s held, retrospectives done, responsibilities agreed)
  • Consequence evidence: what did it produce? Task consequences (deadlines, quality, errors, sales) and human consequences (trust, fairness, workload, wellbeing, voice).
Common mistake

Measuring only results (consequences) and not whether you actually did what you planned (implementation). If results are bad you cannot tell whether the plan was wrong or simply not applied.

Key messages L6
  1. There is no good leadership without sound diagnosis.
  2. Addressing symptoms is not solving problems.
  3. Understand the system as well as the individuals.
  4. Judgement develops through practice, feedback and reflection.
  5. Zooming out should inform a concrete response.
  6. Criteria should align with philosophy and practice.

L7 · Levels of execution

How can leaders choose where and how to intervene, in ways that fit the situation and their philosophy? · LEM: antecedents + styles + cycles

After communicating (L5) and diagnosing and evaluating (L6), the leader must decide where to intervene. The level is the main target of the action.

Relational · the person

One-to-one conversations: clarify expectations, identify obstacles, give feedback, develop responsibility. Combine observation, active listening and an agreement on next steps, then follow through. Examine your own behaviour too; don't assume poor motivation.

"The individual relationship is the fundamental unit of leadership." Related to LMX theory (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995).

Contextual · the conditions

Change what surrounds the work: task sequences, roles, information flows, resources, norms, incentives. If several people repeatedly meet the same obstacle, fix the system.

"Changing systems can produce more lasting change than changing individuals."

Hierarchical · authority

Decisions within legitimate authority: priorities, allocating responsibilities, setting boundaries. Explain the rationale; clarify who decides what. Without authority: seek agreement or refer to the decision-maker.

"Authority is only one of the possible forms of influence."

One problem, three levels

Situation: customer complaints about late replies are rising in a support team.

  • Relational: 1:1 with the two agents with the most delays. You discover one is covering a colleague's queue informally.
  • Contextual: the ticket system assigns tickets randomly, with no visibility of workload. You introduce a shared dashboard and a rotation rule.
  • Hierarchical: you decide that tickets older than 24h are escalated automatically, and announce the rule with its reason.

The levels complement each other. Effectiveness depends on intervening at the level that is actually sustaining the problem.

Delegation and management

Active management means taking responsibility for decisions; it can include shared decision-making and delegation. Passive management means avoiding or delaying needed responsibility. Not intervening also has consequences.

W7 · Bulletproof delegation (8 mechanisms)
  1. Share the goal and the success criterion
  2. A common language
  3. A reporting format
  4. Delegate the analysis, not just the collection (the leader controls the result, not the process)
  5. Paced checkpoints (supervision on a schedule, not on impulse)
  6. Read-back (they restate the goal in their own words)
  7. An escalation rule (what is decided below, what goes up)
  8. Final validation before acting

The "Disney leadership" card game: only the leader knew the goal, the information was with the employees. Lesson: people cannot deliver what they cannot see.

Levels vs. behaviours: where vs. how

The level identifies where to act; the LEM behaviours identify how. Vision, inspiration, instruction, individualisation, support, feedback and management can be used at any level. Choice depends on the leader, the members and the situation: technical capacity, psychological conditions, situational resources. Ask: what can I change, what must I accommodate, what needs another decision-maker?

LevelBehaviours that fit naturally
RelationalIndividualisation, support, instruction, positive feedback, active listening
ContextualDecentralised active management (co-design the process), vision (why the change), instruction
HierarchicalCentralised active management, clear rationale (vision), negative feedback only for breaches of agreed standards

Criteria and review

Separate implementation (did the action occur? responsibilities agreed, reviews done) from consequences (task: timeliness, quality; human: trust, fairness, workload). Criteria aligned, clear, specific, measurable, with a source and a review date.

Key messages L7
  1. The individual relationship remains the fundamental unit of leadership.
  2. Changing systems can produce more lasting change than changing individuals.
  3. Authority is only one of the possible forms of influence.
  4. Involving people in decisions fosters commitment and responsibility.
  5. The absence of intervention also has consequences.
  6. Effectiveness depends on intervening at the right level of the problem.
For your plan

For each main action in your practice, state its level (R/C/H) and the behaviours involved. A strong plan uses all three levels, not only 1:1 conversations.

L8 · Foundations of Motivation II

How can leaders understand what motivates people and create conditions that support the quality and persistence of motivation? · LEM: antecedent factors (psychological favourability)

What motivation is

From Latin movere, to move. Motivation is the process responsible for the intensity, direction and persistence of effort towards a goal (Robbins).

Intensity

How much effort.

Direction

What the effort aims at. Effort must go towards the right priorities.

Persistence

How long it is sustained.

High effort does not guarantee performance if priorities, strategies, skills or resources are inadequate. Motivation comes from the interaction between person and situation, is inferred from behaviour over time, and is not a fixed trait.

Content theories: what people need

Maslow (1943): hierarchy of needs

Unsatisfied needs organise behaviour; basic needs take priority; once substantially satisfied, a need loses force and the next becomes salient. Originally a theory of human development, not of work. Limits: weak empirical support for a strict hierarchy; people pursue several needs at once.

Herzberg (1968): two-factor theory

Motivators (intrinsic to the work)Hygiene factors (context)
Achievement, recognition, interesting or challenging work, responsibility, advancement, growthPolicies, supervision, relationships, pay, working conditions, job security
Present → satisfaction; absent → no satisfactionAdequate → no dissatisfaction; poor → dissatisfaction

Two continua, not one: removing dissatisfaction does not create motivation. A pay rise stops complaints; it does not make boring work engaging.

McClelland (1987): acquired needs

MotiveSeeksOpportunity that fitsRisk
Achievement (nAch)Excellence, improvement, personal responsibility, useful feedback, moderate challengeStretch goals with clear feedbackStar individual performers do not automatically make good managers
Affiliation (nAff)Close relationships, cooperation, belongingTeam roles, onboarding buddiesDifficult feedback becomes uncomfortable
Power (nPow)Influence and impact on othersLeading initiatives, representing the teamCan serve collective goals (institutional) or personal status (personal)

People can have several strong motives. Use the theory to ask which opportunities suit someone, not to label them.

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000)

SDT distinguishes motivation by its quality: the reasons behind action. It is the main motivation theory of the course (and of the LEM's psychological favourability).

Autonomy

Experiencing choice and personal endorsement of what you do. Not the same as independence: you can depend on others and still act autonomously.

Leader: explain the why, offer choices in how, acknowledge feelings, minimise pressure.

Competence

Feeling effective and able to meet challenges.

Leader: optimal challenge, clear structure, informational feedback, visible progress.

Relatedness

Feeling connected, respected and cared for.

Leader: genuine interest, inclusion, a team where people support each other.

The self-determination continuum

Evidence at work (Deci, Olafsen & Ryan, 2017): autonomy-supportive managers → more need satisfaction → more autonomous motivation → better performance, engagement and wellbeing, and less burnout and turnover. Controlling rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation; rewards perceived as informational (acknowledging competence) are less harmful.

Common confusion: extrinsic is not always bad

Identified and integrated regulation are extrinsic (done for an outcome) but autonomous. Most work tasks are not fun; the leader's job is to help people see why they matter (identified), not to make everything intrinsically enjoyable.

Feedback cycles and perceived progress

Useful feedback makes the link between action and improvement visible. A distant target gives direction but little evidence of progress. Feedback = information about performance; recognition = acknowledgement of a contribution. Both should be specific and credible.

Workshop links: feedback

W8 Alignment feedback: "Feedback without an agreement is just an opinion." Agree first (what, how, three concrete rules), then give SBI + A: Situation, Behaviour, Agreement, Impact. Make agreements observable ("only one person speaks at a time", not "communicate well").
W9 Continuous improvement feedback: "The meeting after the game prepares the next game." Ask first, hear the resistance, then argue; SBI on 2–3 key moments; close with Keep · Drop · Add. Three moments, three styles: directive and brief during the task, facilitative after it, private one-to-one when it's about one person's behaviour.

Link to the LEM

Psychological favourability = leader's relationship orientation + members' psychological maturity (confidence and motivation to take responsibility). SDT tells you how to raise it: autonomy (decentralised active management), competence (instruction, positive feedback), relatedness (support, individualisation).

Key messages L8
  1. No single theory explains all motivation.
  2. The quality of motivation matters.
  3. Different people require different approaches.
  4. Autonomy is a powerful source of motivation.
  5. Leaders shape the conditions for motivation rather than creating it directly.
  6. Feedback and perceived progress strengthen competence and sustain motivation.
