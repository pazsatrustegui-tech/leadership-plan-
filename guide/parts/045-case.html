The practical case: method and practice

20% of the grade · "demonstrating ability to apply leadership and motivation concepts to complex organisational situations"

A seven-step method that follows the course

What usually earns marks
  • Precise vocabulary ("decentralised active management", "introjected regulation", "faultline")
  • Separating facts from interpretations
  • Diagnosing before prescribing
  • Acting on the system, not only on individuals
  • Concrete actions and measurable criteria
  • Considering the human consequences and ethics
What usually loses marks
  • Describing theories without applying them
  • "The employee is lazy": attribution error, one explanation
  • Generic solutions ("improve communication", "do team building")
  • Only hierarchical solutions (fire, punish, impose)
  • No way of checking whether the solution worked

Practice case A · The star who became a manager

Case

Marta was the best sales consultant in a software company for four years. Six months ago she was promoted to lead her former team of seven. Since then team sales have dropped 12%. Marta reviews every proposal before it is sent and often rewrites it herself late at night. In meetings she explains how she would close each deal. Two senior consultants, who also applied for her position, have stopped speaking in meetings; one has started to apply for jobs elsewhere. The two newest members say they "never know if they're doing well". The commercial director keeps telling Marta that "numbers are what matter". When asked about her leadership, Marta says: "I want my team to grow and be autonomous."

Model answer (try it first)

Facts: −12% sales; Marta reviews and rewrites all proposals; seniors silent, one job-hunting; newcomers lack feedback; director focuses on numbers only.

Problems, named with concepts:

  • Incongruent cycle (H1): her philosophy says "growth and autonomy"; her practice is micromanagement; her criteria (from the director) are only sales. Her stated philosophy, what she does and how she is judged point in different directions.
  • Suboptimal profile (H2): centralised active management, close to micromanagement; little individualisation, support or positive feedback; instruction given as "how I would do it" instead of a positive sequence.
  • Expertise ≠ leadership (L1): her technical excellence is not translating into leadership. McClelland: high need for achievement, doing it herself.
  • Motivation (SDT): seniors' autonomy is frustrated, so they disengage; newcomers' competence need is unmet because there is no feedback. Relatedness is weakened. Seniors may also carry resentment from the promotion (an LMX "out-group" risk).
  • Situation: the director's results-only view pushes a results-oriented process ("if it works don't fix it") and ignores human outcomes.
  • Partly an adaptive challenge: Marta must change her own assumptions about what her job is.

Alternative interpretations: the drop could also come from market factors. Check pipeline data before concluding it is all leadership (avoid confirmation bias).

Antecedents: technical favourability high (competent team); psychological favourability low (seniors' motivation, newcomers' confidence); situational favourability mixed (pressure from the director).

Interventions:

  • Relational: 1:1s with the two seniors to listen (active listening, validate + redirect), recognise their expertise and give each ownership of a key account or a mentoring role (individualisation, decentralised active management). Fortnightly 1:1s with newcomers using the positive instruction sequence and SBI feedback.
  • Contextual: replace "Marta reviews everything" with clear quality criteria, peer review in pairs (senior + newcomer), and Marta reviewing only proposals above a threshold (responsible delegation with an escalation rule). Weekly team meeting with Keep · Drop · Add.
  • Hierarchical: Marta negotiates with the director a short-term target plus a few human indicators for the next quarter (vision and instruction upwards to improve situational favourability).
  • Marta herself: rewrite her philosophy, practice and criteria so they are congruent; a mentor or coach helps her "go to the balcony".

Criteria: % of proposals sent without Marta's rewrite (target ≥ 70% by month 2); 1:1s held (≥ 90%); seniors' participation in meetings (observation); pulse items on autonomy and "I know how I'm doing" (≥ 4/5 at month 3); retention of the senior; sales recovered to the previous level within two quarters.

Practice case B · The merged warehouse

Case

After a merger, two logistics teams (12 people from the original company, 8 from the acquired one) now share one warehouse under a new supervisor, João. Each group keeps its own procedures, sits on its own side of the warehouse and takes breaks separately. Errors in shipments have doubled. In meetings the original team dominates the discussion; the acquired team stays quiet and later complains among themselves. João has offered a bonus to the team with fewer errors each month. The situation got worse.

Model answer (try it first)

Problems: a group, not a team (no interdependence or shared goal); a faultline along company of origin; stuck in storming with conflict going underground; diversity without inclusion (the acquired team is present but not heard, so tokenism); low psychological safety for the minority; two procedures, which is a contextual problem.

Why the bonus failed: it is external regulation and a transactional exchange that reinforces the faultline: it rewards competition between the two subgroups instead of a shared goal. It treats an adaptive challenge (integration, trust) as a technical one (incentive).

Interventions: hierarchical: one single procedure as a non-negotiable goal, with a clear rationale; replace the competing bonus with a shared team goal. Contextual: mixed shifts and mixed pairs; one shared break area; process design by a mixed working group (decentralised active management); meeting rules: silent input first, rotating facilitation, leader speaks last. Relational: 1:1s with members of the acquired team; validate their concerns; individualise roles to use their expertise. Behaviours: vision ("one warehouse, zero errors"), support, positive feedback on cooperation, instruction on the new procedure.

Criteria: shipment error rate back to pre-merger level within 3 months; % of shifts with mixed pairs; speaking time or participation of both groups in meetings (observer count); psychological safety item with no gap between groups; single procedure documented by week 6.
