Followers are not passive

Leadership is a relationship: followers shape leaders as much as leaders shape followers. Northouse treats followership as a process in which individuals accept the influence of others to achieve a common goal, and stresses that effective followers are active, critical and engaged.

Leader–member exchange (LMX)

Graen and Uhl-Bien (1995): leaders build different-quality relationships with each member. High-quality exchanges (in-group: trust, respect, extra responsibility) predict better performance, satisfaction and commitment; low-quality exchanges (out-group: only the formal contract) produce the opposite. Leaders should try to build high-quality relationships with everyone, and be aware of similarity bias in who ends up "in" (L3). Connects with the relational level in L7.

Follower characteristics in the LEM

The LEM considers members' personal factors (age, gender, education, experience), stable mental factors (personality, goals, values) and dynamic mental factors (coping, confidence, motivation), summarised as technical maturity (competence) and psychological maturity (confidence and willingness to take responsibility). Practical rule from Pro●Leader: negotiate active management with mature members; persuade less mature ones.

Context

Situational characteristics in the LEM: the leader's responsibility (hierarchical level, autonomy, power), the type of organisation, the external environment, culture, goals given to the leader, material and financial conditions. Contingency theories (Fiedler: leader–member relations, task structure, position power; path–goal: task and environment) all point the same way: the same behaviour works differently in different contexts. Examples of contexts that change leadership: remote or hybrid work, crisis, startups vs. bureaucracies, cross-cultural teams, regulated industries.

L10 · Strategy, organisational culture and change

How does leadership connect to the organisation as a whole? · LEM: situation · Integration + practical case (30/11)

Preview based on Robbins, Judge & Vohra (2024) and Northouse (2022). To be updated after the lecture.

Organisational culture

A system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes the organisation from others (Robbins). Schein's three levels:

ArtifactsVisible: office layout, dress, rituals, stories, language
→
Espoused valuesWhat the organisation says it values: mission, codes
→
Basic assumptionsTaken-for-granted beliefs that really drive behaviour

Culture is transmitted through stories, rituals, material symbols and language, and created and sustained by founders, selection, top management behaviour and socialisation. A strong culture increases consistency but can become a barrier to change, diversity and mergers. Leaders shape culture mostly through what they pay attention to, measure, reward and role-model: in LEM terms, through their criteria and practice, not their stated philosophy.

Leading change

Lewin's three steps
  1. Unfreeze: create readiness, reduce resistance
  2. Move / change: new behaviours and processes
  3. Refreeze: stabilise through norms, systems, rewards
Kotter's eight steps
  1. Establish a sense of urgency
  2. Form a guiding coalition
  3. Create a vision
  4. Communicate the vision
  5. Empower others to act; remove obstacles
  6. Create short-term wins
  7. Consolidate improvements
  8. Institutionalise the change in the culture

Resistance to change

Individual sources: habit, security, economic factors, fear of the unknown, selective information processing. Organisational sources: structural inertia, group norms, threat to expertise, power and resources. Ways to reduce it: education and communication, participation, building support and commitment, developing positive relationships, implementing changes fairly, selecting people who accept change. Coercion and manipulation work short term but damage trust.

Connections with earlier lectures

Change is usually an adaptive challenge (L6). Resistance is information: "ask first, hear the resistance, then argue" (W9). Short-term wins = perceived progress (L8). Merging teams (L4) is a change process: faultlines and storming are expected.

Patterns across all workshops
  • Participant ↔ observer roles: the same structure as Pro●Leader's role-play training (leader, follower, observer). Observing is a leadership skill.
  • Practice → feedback → repetition (two rounds): the LEM feedback loop between conceptual and practical cycles.
  • Agreements before evaluation: criteria must be defined before you can judge (L6).
  • Facts, not character: protect people, challenge ideas (psychological safety + standards, L4).
