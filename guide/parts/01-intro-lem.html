1 · The Leadership Efficacy Model (LEM)

Gomes (2014, 2020); Gomes & Morais (2025). The integrative framework used throughout the course, and the structure of the Leadership Plan.

1.1 Leadership and leadership efficacy

Definition · Northouse (2022)

Leadership is "a process whereby an individual influences a group of individuals to achieve a common goal." Four elements: it is a process (not a trait), it involves influence, it happens in groups, and it is about common goals.

Definition · Gomes (2023, 2024), used in Pro●Leader

Leadership is a process of influence exerted on others, sustained on common and positive ideals, that leads everyone involved to a better situation than the one before the leader's influence. This definition builds the consequences into leadership. Three things must happen at the same time:

  1. influence on others (individuals, teams, organisations, communities);
  2. common and positive ideals shared by everyone involved;
  3. a better scenario than the one before.

Leadership efficacy is the impact and consequences of leadership, measured at the individual, group or organisational level (Kaiser et al., 2008). The LEM distinguishes two kinds of outcomes:

Subjective outcomes (implicit impact)

Commitment, loyalty, satisfaction with the leader, trust, cohesion, wellbeing. Psychological, relational and social effects.

Objective outcomes (explicit impact)

Achievement of goals, performance, productivity, profit, income. Behavioural effects: what people actually do and achieve.

Common confusion

Under this definition, a manager who hits targets by burning people out is not fully "effective": the situation is not better for everyone involved. Results alone are not leadership efficacy. This is why L3 asks whether leadership can be effective without ethical legitimacy.

The model at a glance

The LEM proposes that leadership efficacy depends on three sets of factors. Gomes gives each one a body metaphor:

1 · Leadership cycles · the "brain"

What the leader believes (philosophy), does (practice) and evaluates (criteria). The why, the how and the how much.

2 · Leadership styles · the "heart"

The 9 concrete behaviours the leader uses to bring the cycles to life: transformational, transactional, decision-making.

3 · Antecedent factors · the "arms and legs"

Characteristics of the leader, the team members and the situation. They increase or decrease the potential of the plan.

1.2 Leadership cycles: philosophy → practice → criteria

The cycle is a triphasic relation (the model was first called the "Triphasic Model of Leadership Efficacy"): three linked phases, in a fixed logical order.

PhaseDefinition (Gomes, 2020)Question it answersPlan-template question
PhilosophyValues, beliefs, assumptions, attitudes, principles and priorities assumed by the leader, which influence both practice and criteria.Why do I lead? What do I stand for?What is your leadership philosophy? Your most important ideas and goals? Are they important to your team?
PracticeSpecific, observable behaviours the leader adopts to fulfil the philosophy.How do I turn ideas into action?What is your leadership approach? Are your actions important to your team?
CriteriaPersonal and professional indicators that help the leader monitor whether philosophy and practice are being fulfilled.How much change am I producing? How will I know?What are your criteria/indicators? Are they important to your team?

Two levels of the cycle

Conceptual cycle

How the leader intends to lead: the plan on paper. Philosophy, practice and criteria as designed, ideally after listening to the team.

Practical cycle

How the leader actually leads in a real situation: philosophy as transmitted, leadership "in practice" (behaviours perceived by the leader and desired by members), criteria as applied.

The feedback loop connects the two: when reality shows a mismatch, the leader adjusts the plan. Example from the bibliography: the All Blacks coaches valued shared responsibility and autonomy, but adjusted their approach after critical incidents such as poor results and binge-drinking episodes (Hodge et al., 2014). Criteria are what make that adjustment possible: without indicators the leader cannot tell whether to keep or change course.

Example from Gomes (2020), translated to business

Philosophy: "The team comes first. Individuals close deals; teams build accounts."
Practice: set shared goals for each account squad and for the whole sales team; weekly squad meeting to review and recommit.
Criteria: squads receive a weekly dashboard of shared-goal progress; in the Friday meeting goals are adjusted for the next week.
Congruence check (practical cycle): the leader feels people took up the message; members say "we understood the idea, we are known as a team that stands together." Philosophy, practice and criteria line up, so the cycle is congruent.

Writing a good philosophy (Gomes, 2020)

The philosophy does not need to be complex. It should be:

Positive

A stimulating, challenging mission for everyone involved (not only for the leader or the company).

Specific

Concrete and understandable by every member. "Simple but not simplistic."

Personal vocation + ethical

Reflects what the leader genuinely believes and is enthusiastic about, and is acceptable within the ethical and social norms of the context.

Gomes also uses four philosophical lenses to build it:

LensQuestionIn a business leadership philosophy
OntologyWhat does it mean to be a leader?"For me, leading means developing people's potential, not just delivering this quarter."
AxiologyWhich values do I assume?A positive, simple vision that transcends individual interests: "We grow together and nobody wins alone."
EthicsWhat is morally acceptable?"Results never justify misleading clients or each other."
PhenomenologyHow do I experience leading?Personal enthusiasm and optimism about the mission: "I love seeing a junior become autonomous."

Three ways leaders build a philosophy (only one is recommended)

Ideological process ✔

Philosophy → practice → criteria. Starts from reflected values. The recommended, linear route.

Experimental process

Practice → philosophy. "Practice makes the leader": trial and error, repeating what worked in the past without reflecting on principles.

Results-oriented process

Criteria → philosophy. "If it works, don't fix it": good leadership is whatever produced results this time.

Why this matters for the plan

Gomes et al. (2018) interviewed ten elite coaches: full matches between philosophy, practice and criteria appeared in only 21% of cases. Even very successful leaders struggle to connect what they believe with what they do and measure. A plan that shows explicit, traceable links between the three phases is exactly what the model values.

1.3 Leadership styles: the nine behaviours

Styles are specific behaviours used to achieve a leadership goal. The LEM groups nine behaviours into three domains (Gomes & Resende, 2014; measured with the Multidimensional Scale of Leadership).

Optimal vs. suboptimal leadership profile (H2)

The novelty of the LEM is that styles should be combined into an optimal profile, not used one at a time.

Evidence behind it: transformational leadership shows the strongest overall relation with effectiveness (ρ = .44), contingent reward follows closely (.39), and laissez-faire is negative (−.37) (Judge & Piccolo, 2004). Transformational leadership adds explanatory power over transactional leadership: the augmentation effect (Bass, 1985). Effects are stronger on subjective outcomes (satisfaction, commitment) than on objective ones (performance).

Trap: "active management" means different things in different models

In the LEM, active management is about power and decisions: does the leader take responsibility for decisions, and does he or she share them (decentralised) or keep them (centralised)?
In Bass's Full Range model (L2), active management-by-exception is about deviations from standards: does the leader monitor and intervene before mistakes become serious?
LEM passive management ≈ Bass's laissez-faire (avoiding decisions and responsibility).

Negative feedback: alternatives the LEM prefers

When something goes wrong, punishment is not the only option. Gomes suggests disagreement expressed positively (when the action must change) or corrective instruction (when the way the action is performed must change). Negative feedback is reserved for deliberate breaches of previously agreed standards.

1.4 Antecedent factors and favourability

Antecedent factors do not lead by themselves. They moderate the effect of cycles and styles: they work as facilitators (increasing the leader's positive influence) or inhibitors (decreasing it).

FactorPersonalStable mentalDynamic mentalWhat the leader should do
LeaderGender, age, educationPersonality, life goals, beliefs, valuesTolerance of adversity, copingKnow yourself; adjust actions to your own characteristics.
Team membersGender, age, education, experiencePersonality, goals, valuesCoping, motivation, confidenceUnderstand how each person works; adjust to them.
SituationResponsibility in the organisation (hierarchical level, autonomy, power) · type of organisation · external environment (market, competition, regulation) · culture · goals set for the leader · material and financial conditionsMaximise the available resources and opportunities.

Three types of favourability

The antecedents combine into the favourability of conditions for leadership (drawing on Fiedler, Hersey & Blanchard, and Likert):

Technical favourability

Leader: task orientation (value given to mission and goals).
Members: task maturity (competence, knowledge of what must be done).

Raise it with: vision, inspiration, instruction, positive feedback.

Psychological favourability

Leader: relationship orientation (interest in people's needs, expectations, values).
Members: psychological maturity (self-confidence and motivation to accept responsibility).

Raise it with: individualisation, support, decentralised active management.

Situational favourability

Material: resources, budget.
Human: number, experience and maturity of members.
Environmental: market, deadlines, competition.

Raise it with: vision and instruction to convince decision makers; adapting behaviours to members' maturity.

Example

A new team lead inherits six experienced but disengaged engineers, a frozen budget and a hard launch date. Technical favourability is high (competent members). Psychological favourability is low (low motivation to take responsibility). Situational favourability is low (budget, deadline). Adjustment: lean on individualisation and support to rebuild engagement; use decentralised active management to give the seniors ownership of the launch plan; use vision and instruction upwards to negotiate scope with the director.

1.5 The four hypotheses of the LEM

HypothesisStatementIn one line
H1 · Congruence of leadership cyclesEfficacy increases when the conceptual cycle (how the leader intends to lead) matches the practical cycle (how they actually lead), considering both the leader's and the members' perspectives.Do what you say, measure what you value.
H2 · Optimal leadership profileEfficacy increases when congruence is sustained with high transformational leadership, more positive than negative feedback, and active (decentralised) rather than passive management.The right behaviours raise the quality of the cycle.
H3 · Favourability of conditionsEfficacy increases when antecedent factors act as facilitators, or when the leader adopts strategies to minimise the inhibiting ones.Read the leader, the people and the context, and adjust.
H4 · Optimised Congruence Hypothesis (OCHL)Efficacy is highest when the leader combines all three: congruent cycles + optimal profile + consideration of antecedent factors.The whole model together.
Congruence is necessary but not sufficient

A leader can be perfectly congruent around a harmful idea (Bass's pseudo-transformational leader). That is why the LEM judges the quality of the cycle through the styles (H2) and why the definition requires positive ideals and a better situation for everyone.
