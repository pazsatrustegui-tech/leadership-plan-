1 · The Leadership Efficacy Model (LEM)

Gomes (2014, 2020); Gomes & Morais (2025). The integrative framework used throughout the course, and the structure of the Leadership Plan.

1.1 Leadership and leadership efficacy

Definition · Northouse (2022)

Leadership is "a process whereby an individual influences a group of individuals to achieve a common goal." Four elements: it is a process (not a trait), it involves influence, it happens in groups, and it is about common goals.

Definition · Gomes (2023, 2024), used in Pro●Leader

Leadership is a process of influence exerted on others, sustained on common and positive ideals, that leads everyone involved to a better situation than the one before the leader's influence. This definition builds the consequences into leadership. Three things must happen at the same time:

  1. influence on others (individuals, teams, organisations, communities);
  2. common and positive ideals shared by everyone involved;
  3. a better scenario than the one before.

Leadership efficacy is the impact and consequences of leadership, measured at the individual, group or organisational level (Kaiser et al., 2008). The LEM distinguishes two kinds of outcomes:

Subjective outcomes (implicit impact)

Commitment, loyalty, satisfaction with the leader, trust, cohesion, wellbeing. Psychological, relational and social effects.

Objective outcomes (explicit impact)

Achievement of goals, performance, productivity, profit, income. Behavioural effects: what people actually do and achieve.

Common confusion

Under this definition, a manager who hits targets by burning people out is not fully "effective": the situation is not better for everyone involved. Results alone are not leadership efficacy. This is why L3 asks whether leadership can be effective without ethical legitimacy.

The model at a glance

The LEM proposes that leadership efficacy depends on three sets of factors. Gomes gives each one a body metaphor:

1 · Leadership cycles · the "brain"

What the leader believes (philosophy), does (practice) and evaluates (criteria). The why, the how and the how much.

2 · Leadership styles · the "heart"

The 9 concrete behaviours the leader uses to bring the cycles to life: transformational, transactional, decision-making.

3 · Antecedent factors · the "arms and legs"

Characteristics of the leader, the team members and the situation. They increase or decrease the potential of the plan.

1.2 Leadership cycles: philosophy → practice → criteria

The cycle is a triphasic relation (the model was first called the "Triphasic Model of Leadership Efficacy"): three linked phases, in a fixed logical order.

PhaseDefinition (Gomes, 2020)Question it answersPlan-template question
PhilosophyValues, beliefs, assumptions, attitudes, principles and priorities assumed by the leader, which influence both practice and criteria.Why do I lead? What do I stand for?What is your leadership philosophy? Your most important ideas and goals? Are they important to your team?
PracticeSpecific, observable behaviours the leader adopts to fulfil the philosophy.How do I turn ideas into action?What is your leadership approach? Are your actions important to your team?
CriteriaPersonal and professional indicators that help the leader monitor whether philosophy and practice are being fulfilled.How much change am I producing? How will I know?What are your criteria/indicators? Are they important to your team?

Two levels of the cycle

Conceptual cycle

How the leader intends to lead: the plan on paper. Philosophy, practice and criteria as designed, ideally after listening to the team.

Practical cycle

How the leader actually leads in a real situation: philosophy as transmitted, leadership "in practice" (behaviours perceived by the leader and desired by members), criteria as applied.

The feedback loop connects the two: when reality shows a mismatch, the leader adjusts the plan. Example from the bibliography: the All Blacks coaches valued shared responsibility and autonomy, but adjusted their approach after critical incidents such as poor results and binge-drinking episodes (Hodge et al., 2014). Criteria are what make that adjustment possible: without indicators the leader cannot tell whether to keep or change course.

Example from Gomes (2020), translated to business

Philosophy: "The team comes first. Individuals close deals; teams build accounts."
Practice: set shared goals for each account squad and for the whole sales team; weekly squad meeting to review and recommit.
Criteria: squads receive a weekly dashboard of shared-goal progress; in the Friday meeting goals are adjusted for the next week.
Congruence check (practical cycle): the leader feels people took up the message; members say "we understood the idea, we are known as a team that stands together." Philosophy, practice and criteria line up, so the cycle is congruent.

Writing a good philosophy (Gomes, 2020)

The philosophy does not need to be complex. It should be:

Positive

A stimulating, challenging mission for everyone involved (not only for the leader or the company).

Specific

Concrete and understandable by every member. "Simple but not simplistic."

Personal vocation + ethical

Reflects what the leader genuinely believes and is enthusiastic about, and is acceptable within the ethical and social norms of the context.

Gomes also uses four philosophical lenses to build it:

LensQuestionIn a business leadership philosophy
OntologyWhat does it mean to be a leader?"For me, leading means developing people's potential, not just delivering this quarter."
AxiologyWhich values do I assume?A positive, simple vision that transcends individual interests: "We grow together and nobody wins alone."
EthicsWhat is morally acceptable?"Results never justify misleading clients or each other."
PhenomenologyHow do I experience leading?Personal enthusiasm and optimism about the mission: "I love seeing a junior become autonomous."

Three ways leaders build a philosophy (only one is recommended)

Ideological process ✔

Philosophy → practice → criteria. Starts from reflected values. The recommended, linear route.

Experimental process

Practice → philosophy. "Practice makes the leader": trial and error, repeating what worked in the past without reflecting on principles.

Results-oriented process

Criteria → philosophy. "If it works, don't fix it": good leadership is whatever produced results this time.

Why this matters for the plan

Gomes et al. (2018) interviewed ten elite coaches: full matches between philosophy, practice and criteria appeared in only 21% of cases. Even very successful leaders struggle to connect what they believe with what they do and measure. A plan that shows explicit, traceable links between the three phases is exactly what the model values.

Optimal vs. suboptimal leadership profile (H2)

The novelty of the LEM is that styles should be combined into an optimal profile, not used one at a time.

Evidence behind it: transformational leadership shows the strongest overall relation with effectiveness (ρ = .44), contingent reward follows closely (.39), and laissez-faire is negative (−.37) (Judge & Piccolo, 2004). Transformational leadership adds explanatory power over transactional leadership: the augmentation effect (Bass, 1985). Effects are stronger on subjective outcomes (satisfaction, commitment) than on objective ones (performance).

Trap: "active management" means different things in different models

In the LEM, active management is about power and decisions: does the leader take responsibility for decisions, and does he or she share them (decentralised) or keep them (centralised)?
In Bass's Full Range model (L2), active management-by-exception is about deviations from standards: does the leader monitor and intervene before mistakes become serious?
LEM passive management ≈ Bass's laissez-faire (avoiding decisions and responsibility).

Negative feedback: alternatives the LEM prefers

When something goes wrong, punishment is not the only option. Gomes suggests disagreement expressed positively (when the action must change) or corrective instruction (when the way the action is performed must change). Negative feedback is reserved for deliberate breaches of previously agreed standards.

1.4 Antecedent factors and favourability

Antecedent factors do not lead by themselves. They moderate the effect of cycles and styles: they work as facilitators (increasing the leader's positive influence) or inhibitors (decreasing it).

FactorPersonalStable mentalDynamic mentalWhat the leader should do
LeaderGender, age, educationPersonality, life goals, beliefs, valuesTolerance of adversity, copingKnow yourself; adjust actions to your own characteristics.

Three types of favourability

The antecedents combine into the favourability of conditions for leadership (drawing on Fiedler, Hersey & Blanchard, and Likert):

Technical favourability

Leader: task orientation (value given to mission and goals).
Members: task maturity (competence, knowledge of what must be done).

Raise it with: vision, inspiration, instruction, positive feedback.

Psychological favourability

Leader: relationship orientation (interest in people's needs, expectations, values).
Members: psychological maturity (self-confidence and motivation to accept responsibility).

Raise it with: individualisation, support, decentralised active management.

Situational favourability

Material: resources, budget.
Human: number, experience and maturity of members.
Environmental: market, deadlines, competition.

Raise it with: vision and instruction to convince decision makers; adapting behaviours to members' maturity.

Example

A new team lead inherits six experienced but disengaged engineers, a frozen budget and a hard launch date. Technical favourability is high (competent members). Psychological favourability is low (low motivation to take responsibility). Situational favourability is low (budget, deadline). Adjustment: lean on individualisation and support to rebuild engagement; use decentralised active management to give the seniors ownership of the launch plan; use vision and instruction upwards to negotiate scope with the director.

1.5 The four hypotheses of the LEM

HypothesisStatementIn one line
H1 · Congruence of leadership cyclesEfficacy increases when the conceptual cycle (how the leader intends to lead) matches the practical cycle (how they actually lead), considering both the leader's and the members' perspectives.Do what you say, measure what you value.
H2 · Optimal leadership profileEfficacy increases when congruence is sustained with high transformational leadership, more positive than negative feedback, and active (decentralised) rather than passive management.The right behaviours raise the quality of the cycle.
H3 · Favourability of conditionsEfficacy increases when antecedent factors act as facilitators, or when the leader adopts strategies to minimise the inhibiting ones.Read the leader, the people and the context, and adjust.
H4 · Optimised Congruence Hypothesis (OCHL)Efficacy is highest when the leader combines all three: congruent cycles + optimal profile + consideration of antecedent factors.The whole model together.
Congruence is necessary but not sufficient

A leader can be perfectly congruent around a harmful idea (Bass's pseudo-transformational leader). That is why the LEM judges the quality of the cycle through the styles (H2) and why the definition requires positive ideals and a better situation for everyone.

L1 · Foundations of Leadership and Motivation I

What is leadership and why does it matter? · LEM: general overview

Leadership is influence, not position

Many people associate leadership with authority, hierarchy, charisma or a job title. The course rejects that view: leadership exists whenever someone influences other people's behaviour, attitudes, decisions, motivation and commitment towards a common goal. It often happens before people hold a formal leadership position. A junior analyst who gets the team to adopt a better process is leading; a director who only signs approvals may not be.

Assigned leadership

Based on a formal position (team lead, director). Authority is given by the organisation.

Emergent leadership

Based on how others respond: people come to see someone as a leader through their behaviour, regardless of title (Northouse, 2022).

Influence and the five bases of power (French & Raven, 1959)

Power is the capacity to influence; influence is power in use. Power itself is neutral: its consequences depend on how it is used (L3).

Personal power (expert, referent) tends to produce commitment; position power (legitimate, reward, coercive) tends to produce compliance. Effective leaders combine them and rely mostly on personal power.

Leadership vs. management (Kotter, 1990)

Kotter argues they are complementary, not opposed: management copes with complexity, leadership copes with change. Organisations need both.

Example

Launching a new product line: management builds the Gantt chart, budget and staffing; leadership explains why the launch matters, gets sales and engineering to believe in it, and keeps people going when the first version fails.

Leadership effectiveness

Effectiveness is the positive and sustainable impact leadership creates on individuals, teams, organisations and outcomes (Gomes, 2020). Kaiser, Hogan and Craig (2008) stress that effectiveness should be judged by the performance of the team or organisation the leader is responsible for, not only by how much the leader is liked or promoted, and that failure rates among managers are high: leadership matters for the fate of organisations.

Leadership and motivation

Motivation drives effort, persistence, engagement and commitment. Leaders cannot inject motivation; they shape the conditions under which people work and develop. Self-Determination Theory (Deci, Olafsen & Ryan, 2017) says motivation depends on three basic needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness (developed in L8).

Motivation is both a consequence and a condition of leadership

Consequence: good leadership produces motivated people. Condition: motivated followers make leadership easier (psychological favourability in the LEM). It is a loop.

Key messages L1
  1. Leadership is influence, not position.
  2. Technical excellence does not automatically make an effective leader.
  3. Leadership exists to mobilise (motivate) people towards shared goals.
  4. Efficacy depends on the interaction of cycles, styles and the characteristics of leader, followers and context.
  5. Motivation is both a consequence and a condition of leadership.
  6. Leadership is a skill that can be developed, not something you have or lack.
For your plan

The "My leadership story" exercise (leaders you admire) is raw material for your philosophy: what did those leaders believe, do and measure? Which of their sources of power do you want to rely on?

L2 · Foundations of Leadership II: the theories

How has leadership research tried to explain effectiveness? · LEM: overview + leadership cycles

Models are simplified representations of a complex reality: they help to understand, diagnose, predict and improve. No theory explains everything; each one answers a different question. The LEM integrates them.

Leadership emergence vs. leadership effectiveness

Emergence

The extent to which a person is perceived as a leader by others. Who gets chosen, noticed, followed.

Effectiveness

The extent to which leadership produces positive and sustainable outcomes for people, teams and results (Kaiser et al., 2008).

Example

The most talkative, confident student is usually chosen to lead the group project (emergence). Whether the project is good and the group stays united is a different question (effectiveness). Extraversion predicts emergence more than effectiveness.

Trait approach

Assumption: some stable personal characteristics make people more likely to become leaders. Classic traits: intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, sociability (Northouse, 2022). Modern research uses the Big Five.

Big Five traitCorrelation with leadership (Judge et al., 2002)Reading

In the LEM, traits survive as leader characteristics within the antecedent factors.

Behavioural approach

Assumption: effectiveness depends on what leaders do. Because behaviours can be trained, leadership can be learned. This is the foundation of leadership development, Pro●Leader included.

Task-oriented (initiating structure)

Organising work, defining roles, setting goals and standards, scheduling. Helps people achieve objectives.

Relationship-oriented (consideration)

Respect, trust, attention to wellbeing, making people comfortable. Focuses on people.

Ohio State and Michigan studies, and Blake and Mouton's Managerial Grid (high task + high people = "team management"), all conclude that effective leaders balance both. DeRue et al. (2011) found that behaviours explain more variance in effectiveness than traits, and that behaviours partly mediate the effect of traits: traits → behaviours → effectiveness. Together they explain at least 31% of the variance.

Limitation: no universal "best behaviour" was found; the same behaviour works in one situation and fails in another. That opened the door to contingency theories.

Transformational and contingent reward are strongly correlated (.80): effective leaders use both.

L3 · Ethical leadership and diversity

Can leadership be considered effective if it lacks ethical legitimacy? · LEM: leadership cycles

No leadership is ethically neutral

Because leadership is influence, it always affects other people's opportunities, wellbeing, development and decisions. That creates ethical responsibility. Ethical leadership is the responsible exercise of influence through behaviour consistent with integrity, fairness, honesty and respect. Ethical leaders behave ethically and promote ethical conduct in others: they act as a "moral person" and a "moral manager" (Brown & Treviño, 2006).

Leadership legitimacy

The perception that a leader's influence, decisions and actions are appropriate, fair and acceptable. People trust and follow leaders they see as fair and responsible, so ethics feeds legitimacy and legitimacy feeds influence.

Example: "ethics versus success"

Morais, Abrams and Randsley de Moura (2020) show that people are more willing to accept unethical behaviour from leaders who bring success. The danger: results can "buy" tolerance for misconduct in the short term, while trust and sustainability erode. Think of sales leaders who hit targets with aggressive mis-selling until a scandal breaks.

Power

Power is the capacity to influence people, decisions, resources and outcomes. It is neither ethical nor unethical in itself. The lecture's message: power can improve or deteriorate judgement. It gives the ability to act, but it can also reduce perspective-taking and make leaders less attentive to others' views.

Ethical dilemmas: right vs. right

A dilemma occurs when two or more legitimate values point to different actions. It is not "right vs. wrong" but "right vs. right" (Ciulla, 2020).

TensionExample
Loyalty vs. fairnessPromote a loyal long-time team member or the stronger external candidate?
Individual vs. collectiveGrant one person's flexible-hours request when it increases others' workload?
Truth vs. careTell the team now about possible layoffs, or wait until it is certain?
Short term vs. long termMeet the quarter's target or invest in training that will pay off next year?

Ethical leadership requires judgement, not just good intentions: making the competing values explicit, considering who is affected, and explaining the reasoning.

Cognitive biases (Kahneman, 2011)

Leaders decide with incomplete information and rely on heuristics (mental shortcuts). Shortcuts are useful but produce systematic errors: biases. Good intentions do not protect you from them.

Diversity vs. inclusion

Diversity: who is present

Differences among people: demographic, experiential, professional, cognitive. Gives access to more perspectives, information and problem-solving approaches.

Inclusion: who participates

The extent to which people are respected, heard, able to participate and to influence decisions (Shore et al., 2018). Combines belonging with being valued for one's uniqueness.

"Diversity without inclusion generates little value": a team can look diverse while only the same two people decide.

Psychological safety (Edmondson)

A shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking: people can ask questions, admit mistakes, raise concerns and challenge assumptions without fear of humiliation, punishment or rejection. It does not mean lower standards (see the 2×2 in L4).

Ethics through the LEM: congruence

Ethical leadership in the LEM means congruence between what you believe (philosophy), what you do (practice) and how you evaluate success (criteria). A leader who preaches "people first" but evaluates only revenue is incongruent, and people notice. Incongruence destroys trust and legitimacy.

Key messages L3
  1. There is no ethically neutral leadership.
  2. Ethical leadership requires judgement, not merely good intentions.
  3. Power can improve or deteriorate judgement.
  4. Information processing is vulnerable to biases.
  5. Diversity without inclusion generates little value.
  6. Psychological safety does not mean lower standards.
  7. Sustainable effectiveness requires ethical legitimacy.
For your plan

State an explicit ethical commitment in your philosophy, and include at least one human criterion (fairness, trust, voice), not only task criteria. Name the bias you are most prone to and how your practice protects against it.

L4 · Team development: from groups to high-performing teams

How do leaders turn a group of people into a high-performing team? · LEM: antecedent factors (followers and context)

Four guiding questions of the lecture: who belonged, who supported whom, who could disagree or fail without cost, and who thought differently and what happened to them? The lecture closed with the individual 12-week plan for a newly merged team: the first draft of the final project.

Group vs. team

Group
  • Members answer for their own work
  • Individual goals
  • Result = sum of individual contributions
  • Individual accountability
Team (Katzenbach & Smith, 1993)

"A small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common purpose, set of performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable."

Interdependence + shared goals + mutual accountability.

Not a law

The model came from small, mostly therapy and laboratory groups: it is a shared language, not a law. Stages are not linear: a new member, a new goal or a crisis can send a team back to storming.

Groupthink (Janis, 1972)

The wish for agreement overrides a realistic appraisal of alternatives.

More likely with
  • High cohesion
  • Isolation from outside views
  • A very directive leader
  • Time pressure
  • No method for deciding
Warning signs
  • Illusion of invulnerability
  • Illusion of unanimity
  • Self-censorship of doubts
  • Pressure on dissenters
  • "Mindguards" shielding the leader from bad news
Antidotes
  • Leader speaks last
  • Rotating devil's advocate
  • Pre-mortem: "a year from now we failed. Why?"
  • Silent input before discussion
  • Outside challenge; second-chance meeting

Empirical support for the full model is mixed: use it as a lens.

Diversity in teams (van Knippenberg et al., 2004)

Gains
  • More perspectives and information
  • Better analysis of complex problems
  • Natural brake on groupthink
  • Members prepare more because they expect disagreement (Phillips et al., 2009)
Costs
  • More coordination effort
  • More conflict early on
  • Faultlines: subgroups forming around shared attributes (Lau & Murnighan, 1998), e.g. "the ex-Company A people" vs "the ex-Company B people"
  • Tokenism: present but not heard

More diversity usually means more storming. That is the price of the gain, not a sign of failure. Diversity pays off only with inclusion, psychological safety and a good decision process.

Psychological safety × accountability (Edmondson, 2012, 2018)

L5 · Communication, feedback and conflict management

Can leaders manage conflict in ways that support both performance and the quality of relationships? · LEM: antecedent factors + leadership styles

The nature and sources of conflict

Conflict arises when people perceive that their interests, goals, values or expectations are incompatible. It is inevitable and does not mean the leader failed. Its consequences depend on its nature, context and management.

Task conflict (about the work)

Disagreement about ideas, methods, priorities. At moderate levels it can improve decisions: it surfaces concerns and alternatives.

Relationship conflict (about the people)

Personal attacks, tension, resentment. Almost always damages cooperation and performance (Robbins & Judge). Process conflict (who does what) sits in between.

SourceConcernsExample
InterestsWhat people need or wantTwo managers both want the only available developer.
ValuesWhat people consider important or acceptable"Quality first" vs "ship fast and fix later".
PerceptionsHow people interpret events and behaviourOne reads a short email as rude; the sender meant efficient.
StructuralUnclear roles, competing priorities, limited resources, unmet expectationsSales promises dates that operations was never asked about.

Identifying the source lets the leader address the underlying issue rather than only its visible expression.

Communication Efficacy Model (Gomes, 2023)

Communication is the transmission of information, thoughts, opinions and ideas between two or more people, verbally or non-verbally. The model links:

Communication profiles

Characteristic patterns of behaviour (not fixed personality types) in how people express ideas, guide action and relate to others. They come in mirrored pairs:

FocusPositive profileNegative profile
Vision / futureOptimistic idealist: communicates a shared positive vision; expresses disagreement assertively. "This is hard, but here's why we can do it. I disagree with the timing, and here's my reason."Pessimistic idealist: communicates fear and pessimism; discourages initiative and innovation. "These projects always fail here."
Instruction / correctionPositive technical: constructive instructions focused on what can be done. "Start with the summary, then the data."Negative technical: instructions focused on what others should not do; restricts initiative. "Don't send it like that. Don't use that template."
RelationshipEncourager: promotes understanding, recognition, support and mutual trust.Discourager: disregards or punishes others' ideas, progress or performance.
Profiles vs. conflict styles

Conflict management styles describe how you approach a disagreement. Communication profiles describe how you communicate, inside and outside disagreements. A leader can collaborate (style) while speaking as a discourager (profile).

Mapping profiles to the LEM

Optimistic idealist ≈ vision + inspiration · Positive technical ≈ instruction · Encourager ≈ support + positive feedback · the negative profiles ≈ the suboptimal profile.

Conflict management styles (Thomas–Kilmann)

Styles differ in assertiveness (pursuing your own concerns) and cooperativeness (attending to the other's concerns).

Active listening, empathy, validate and redirect

Active listening

Deliberately attending to the message and checking it was understood: open questions, paraphrasing, clarifying, summarising. It does not require agreement. Seek first to understand, then to be understood.

Empathy

Seeking to understand the other's perspective and emotional experience. It helps identify obstacles and sustain cooperation while keeping expectations and accountability.

Validate + redirect

Validation acknowledges the concern; redirection moves to clarification, expectations, options or a next step. Validation is not agreement.

Validate → redirect, in practice

Employee: "This is the third time the requirements change. I'm fed up."
Validate: "I can see why it feels frustrating, especially after all the work you already put in."
Redirect: "Let's clarify exactly what changed, what can be reused, and agree a realistic next step. I'll also raise the change process with the client lead."

W6 Five levels of listening (Covey): ignoring → pretending → selective → attentive/active → empathic. Every level can be a conscious choice; the real risk is the gap between the level you think you're at and the one you're actually at.

Key messages L5
  1. The problem is not conflict itself but how it is managed.
  2. Listening is a leadership skill.
  3. Validation does not mean agreement.
  4. Communication shapes the quality of relationships.
  5. Empathy can be a tool for improving performance.
For your plan

Say how you will handle disagreement (which style by default, when you will switch) and how you will evaluate a conflict as "well managed": practical outcome + relationship quality + alignment with your philosophy.

L6 · Leadership criteria: zooming out and sound judgement

How can zooming out help leaders define meaningful criteria, in line with their philosophy, followers and context? · LEM: leadership cycles (criteria) + antecedents + styles

Sound judgement

The ability to interpret situations carefully and make reasoned decisions with the available evidence. It involves separating observations from assumptions, considering alternative explanations and recognising uncertainty. It can be developed through reflection, feedback and reviewing decisions and their consequences.

Technical vs. adaptive challenges (Heifetz & Laurie, 1997)

Technical challengeAdaptive challenge
NatureClear problem, known solutionRequires people to learn and change assumptions, priorities, behaviours or relationships
Who solves itExperts, procedures, authorityThe people with the problem; the leader mobilises them
ExampleThe new software crashes on export: IT patches it.Teams won't share client information because the bonus system rewards individual sales.
Classic mistakeTreating an adaptive challenge as technical: sending everyone on a "collaboration training" when the incentive system punishes collaboration. Expertise alone cannot produce the learning adaptive change needs. Many situations mix both.

Zooming out: the balcony and the dance floor

Zooming out means stepping back from immediate demands to see relationships, recurring patterns and longer-term implications, then returning with a better-informed response. Heifetz's metaphor: on the dance floor you only see your partner; from the balcony you see the patterns of the whole room. Leaders need both: closeness to people's experience and enough distance to question first interpretations. Reflection, mentoring and decision reviews support this movement.

Zoom in

The individual and the moment: what is this person doing, feeling, needing right now? Also zooming in on yourself (self-awareness).

Zoom out

The system: roles, incentives, workflows, culture, history. If several people hit the same obstacle, the problem is probably the system.

Workshop link

W2–W3 "Zoom in / Zoom out": zoom in on yourself (self-awareness, the Leadership Orientations questionnaire), then zoom out to the system. The "planning vs on-sight navigation" staircase (plan ahead → observe reality → trial and error → practice → process → discipline → improvement) shows that leadership improves through cycles of planning and correcting against reality, which is the LEM feedback loop.

Leadership evaluation criteria

Criteria are explicit indicators used to assess whether, or to what extent, the intended leadership practices are implemented, and with what consequences. Good criteria are:

QualityMeaningWeakStrong
AlignedFollows from your philosophy and practicePhilosophy "people first"; criterion "revenue +10%" only"Each team member has a development goal agreed in a 1:1 by week 4"
ClearUnambiguous meaning"Better communication""Every decision from the Monday meeting is written down with an owner and a date"

Criteria should also be meaningful to the people affected, have an identified evidence source (survey, log, dashboard, observation, 1:1 notes) and a review time (weekly, monthly, end of quarter).

Two kinds of evidence (L6–L7)
  • Implementation evidence: did the intended action happen? (1:1s held, retrospectives done, responsibilities agreed)
  • Consequence evidence: what did it produce? Task consequences (deadlines, quality, errors, sales) and human consequences (trust, fairness, workload, wellbeing, voice).
Common mistake

Measuring only results (consequences) and not whether you actually did what you planned (implementation). If results are bad you cannot tell whether the plan was wrong or simply not applied.

Key messages L6
  1. There is no good leadership without sound diagnosis.
  2. Addressing symptoms is not solving problems.
  3. Understand the system as well as the individuals.
  4. Judgement develops through practice, feedback and reflection.
  5. Zooming out should inform a concrete response.
  6. Criteria should align with philosophy and practice.

L7 · Levels of execution

How can leaders choose where and how to intervene, in ways that fit the situation and their philosophy? · LEM: antecedents + styles + cycles

After communicating (L5) and diagnosing and evaluating (L6), the leader must decide where to intervene. The level is the main target of the action.

Relational · the person

One-to-one conversations: clarify expectations, identify obstacles, give feedback, develop responsibility. Combine observation, active listening and an agreement on next steps, then follow through. Examine your own behaviour too; don't assume poor motivation.

"The individual relationship is the fundamental unit of leadership." Related to LMX theory (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995).

Contextual · the conditions

Change what surrounds the work: task sequences, roles, information flows, resources, norms, incentives. If several people repeatedly meet the same obstacle, fix the system.

"Changing systems can produce more lasting change than changing individuals."

Hierarchical · authority

Decisions within legitimate authority: priorities, allocating responsibilities, setting boundaries. Explain the rationale; clarify who decides what. Without authority: seek agreement or refer to the decision-maker.

"Authority is only one of the possible forms of influence."

One problem, three levels

Situation: customer complaints about late replies are rising in a support team.

  • Relational: 1:1 with the two agents with the most delays. You discover one is covering a colleague's queue informally.
  • Contextual: the ticket system assigns tickets randomly, with no visibility of workload. You introduce a shared dashboard and a rotation rule.
  • Hierarchical: you decide that tickets older than 24h are escalated automatically, and announce the rule with its reason.

The levels complement each other. Effectiveness depends on intervening at the level that is actually sustaining the problem.

Delegation and management

Active management means taking responsibility for decisions; it can include shared decision-making and delegation. Passive management means avoiding or delaying needed responsibility. Not intervening also has consequences.

W7 · Bulletproof delegation (8 mechanisms)
  1. Share the goal and the success criterion
  2. A common language
  3. A reporting format
  4. Delegate the analysis, not just the collection (the leader controls the result, not the process)
  5. Paced checkpoints (supervision on a schedule, not on impulse)
  6. Read-back (they restate the goal in their own words)
  7. An escalation rule (what is decided below, what goes up)
  8. Final validation before acting

The "Disney leadership" card game: only the leader knew the goal, the information was with the employees. Lesson: people cannot deliver what they cannot see.

Levels vs. behaviours: where vs. how

The level identifies where to act; the LEM behaviours identify how. Vision, inspiration, instruction, individualisation, support, feedback and management can be used at any level. Choice depends on the leader, the members and the situation: technical capacity, psychological conditions, situational resources. Ask: what can I change, what must I accommodate, what needs another decision-maker?

LevelBehaviours that fit naturally
RelationalIndividualisation, support, instruction, positive feedback, active listening
ContextualDecentralised active management (co-design the process), vision (why the change), instruction
HierarchicalCentralised active management, clear rationale (vision), negative feedback only for breaches of agreed standards

Criteria and review

Separate implementation (did the action occur? responsibilities agreed, reviews done) from consequences (task: timeliness, quality; human: trust, fairness, workload). Criteria aligned, clear, specific, measurable, with a source and a review date.

Key messages L7
  1. The individual relationship remains the fundamental unit of leadership.
  2. Changing systems can produce more lasting change than changing individuals.
  3. Authority is only one of the possible forms of influence.
  4. Involving people in decisions fosters commitment and responsibility.
  5. The absence of intervention also has consequences.
  6. Effectiveness depends on intervening at the right level of the problem.
For your plan

For each main action in your practice, state its level (R/C/H) and the behaviours involved. A strong plan uses all three levels, not only 1:1 conversations.

L8 · Foundations of Motivation II

How can leaders understand what motivates people and create conditions that support the quality and persistence of motivation? · LEM: antecedent factors (psychological favourability)

What motivation is

From Latin movere, to move. Motivation is the process responsible for the intensity, direction and persistence of effort towards a goal (Robbins).

Intensity

How much effort.

Direction

What the effort aims at. Effort must go towards the right priorities.

Persistence

How long it is sustained.

High effort does not guarantee performance if priorities, strategies, skills or resources are inadequate. Motivation comes from the interaction between person and situation, is inferred from behaviour over time, and is not a fixed trait.

Content theories: what people need

Maslow (1943): hierarchy of needs

Unsatisfied needs organise behaviour; basic needs take priority; once substantially satisfied, a need loses force and the next becomes salient. Originally a theory of human development, not of work. Limits: weak empirical support for a strict hierarchy; people pursue several needs at once.

Herzberg (1968): two-factor theory

Motivators (intrinsic to the work)Hygiene factors (context)
Achievement, recognition, interesting or challenging work, responsibility, advancement, growthPolicies, supervision, relationships, pay, working conditions, job security
Present → satisfaction; absent → no satisfactionAdequate → no dissatisfaction; poor → dissatisfaction

Two continua, not one: removing dissatisfaction does not create motivation. A pay rise stops complaints; it does not make boring work engaging.

McClelland (1987): acquired needs

MotiveSeeksOpportunity that fitsRisk
Achievement (nAch)Excellence, improvement, personal responsibility, useful feedback, moderate challengeStretch goals with clear feedbackStar individual performers do not automatically make good managers
Affiliation (nAff)Close relationships, cooperation, belongingTeam roles, onboarding buddiesDifficult feedback becomes uncomfortable
Power (nPow)Influence and impact on othersLeading initiatives, representing the teamCan serve collective goals (institutional) or personal status (personal)

People can have several strong motives. Use the theory to ask which opportunities suit someone, not to label them.

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000)

SDT distinguishes motivation by its quality: the reasons behind action. It is the main motivation theory of the course (and of the LEM's psychological favourability).

Autonomy

Experiencing choice and personal endorsement of what you do. Not the same as independence: you can depend on others and still act autonomously.

Leader: explain the why, offer choices in how, acknowledge feelings, minimise pressure.

Competence

Feeling effective and able to meet challenges.

Leader: optimal challenge, clear structure, informational feedback, visible progress.

Relatedness

Feeling connected, respected and cared for.

Leader: genuine interest, inclusion, a team where people support each other.

The self-determination continuum

Evidence at work (Deci, Olafsen & Ryan, 2017): autonomy-supportive managers → more need satisfaction → more autonomous motivation → better performance, engagement and wellbeing, and less burnout and turnover. Controlling rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation; rewards perceived as informational (acknowledging competence) are less harmful.

Common confusion: extrinsic is not always bad

Identified and integrated regulation are extrinsic (done for an outcome) but autonomous. Most work tasks are not fun; the leader's job is to help people see why they matter (identified), not to make everything intrinsically enjoyable.

Feedback cycles and perceived progress

Useful feedback makes the link between action and improvement visible. A distant target gives direction but little evidence of progress. Feedback = information about performance; recognition = acknowledgement of a contribution. Both should be specific and credible.

Workshop links: feedback

W8 Alignment feedback: "Feedback without an agreement is just an opinion." Agree first (what, how, three concrete rules), then give SBI + A: Situation, Behaviour, Agreement, Impact. Make agreements observable ("only one person speaks at a time", not "communicate well").
W9 Continuous improvement feedback: "The meeting after the game prepares the next game." Ask first, hear the resistance, then argue; SBI on 2–3 key moments; close with Keep · Drop · Add. Three moments, three styles: directive and brief during the task, facilitative after it, private one-to-one when it's about one person's behaviour.

Link to the LEM

Psychological favourability = leader's relationship orientation + members' psychological maturity (confidence and motivation to take responsibility). SDT tells you how to raise it: autonomy (decentralised active management), competence (instruction, positive feedback), relatedness (support, individualisation).

Key messages L8
  1. No single theory explains all motivation.
  2. The quality of motivation matters.
  3. Different people require different approaches.
  4. Autonomy is a powerful source of motivation.
  5. Leaders shape the conditions for motivation rather than creating it directly.
  6. Feedback and perceived progress strengthen competence and sustain motivation.

Leader–member exchange (LMX)

Graen and Uhl-Bien (1995): leaders build different-quality relationships with each member. L10 · Strategy, organisational culture and change

How does leadership connect to the organisation as a whole? · LEM: situation · Integration + practical case (30/11)

Preview based on Robbins, Judge & Vohra (2024) and Northouse (2022). To be updated after the lecture.

Organisational culture

A system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes the organisation from others (Robbins). Schein's three levels:

ArtifactsVisible: office layout, dress, rituals, stories, language
→
Espoused valuesWhat the organisation says it values: mission, codes
→
Basic assumptionsTaken-for-granted beliefs that really drive behaviour

Culture is transmitted through stories, rituals, material symbols and language, and created and sustained by founders, selection, top management behaviour and socialisation. A strong culture increases consistency but can become a barrier to change, diversity and mergers. Leaders shape culture mostly through what they pay attention to, measure, reward and role-model: in LEM terms, through their criteria and practice, not their stated philosophy.

Leading change

Lewin's three steps
  1. Unfreeze: create readiness, reduce resistance
  2. Move / change: new behaviours and processes
  3. Refreeze: stabilise through norms, systems, rewards
Kotter's eight steps
  1. Establish a sense of urgency
  2. Form a guiding coalition
  3. Create a vision
  4. Communicate the vision
  5. Empower others to act; remove obstacles
  6. Create short-term wins
  7. Consolidate improvements
  8. Institutionalise the change in the culture

Resistance to change

Individual sources: habit, security, economic factors, fear of the unknown, selective information processing. Organisational sources: structural inertia, group norms, threat to expertise, power and resources. Ways to reduce it: education and communication, participation, building support and commitment, developing positive relationships, implementing changes fairly, selecting people who accept change. Coercion and manipulation work short term but damage trust.

Connections with earlier lectures

Change is usually an adaptive challenge (L6). Resistance is information: "ask first, hear the resistance, then argue" (W9). Short-term wins = perceived progress (L8). Merging teams (L4) is a change process: faultlines and storming are expected.

The practical case: method and practice

20% of the grade · "demonstrating ability to apply leadership and motivation concepts to complex organisational situations"

A seven-step method that follows the course

What usually earns marks
  • Precise vocabulary ("decentralised active management", "introjected regulation", "faultline")
  • Separating facts from interpretations
  • Diagnosing before prescribing
  • Acting on the system, not only on individuals
  • Concrete actions and measurable criteria
  • Considering the human consequences and ethics
What usually loses marks
  • Describing theories without applying them
  • "The employee is lazy": attribution error, one explanation
  • Generic solutions ("improve communication", "do team building")
  • Only hierarchical solutions (fire, punish, impose)
  • No way of checking whether the solution worked

Practice case A · The star who became a manager

Case

Marta was the best sales consultant in a software company for four years. Six months ago she was promoted to lead her former team of seven. Since then team sales have dropped 12%. Marta reviews every proposal before it is sent and often rewrites it herself late at night. In meetings she explains how she would close each deal. Two senior consultants, who also applied for her position, have stopped speaking in meetings; one has started to apply for jobs elsewhere. The two newest members say they "never know if they're doing well". The commercial director keeps telling Marta that "numbers are what matter". When asked about her leadership, Marta says: "I want my team to grow and be autonomous."

Model answer (try it first)

Facts: −12% sales; Marta reviews and rewrites all proposals; seniors silent, one job-hunting; newcomers lack feedback; director focuses on numbers only.

Problems, named with concepts:

  • Incongruent cycle (H1): her philosophy says "growth and autonomy"; her practice is micromanagement; her criteria (from the director) are only sales. Her stated philosophy, what she does and how she is judged point in different directions.
  • Suboptimal profile (H2): centralised active management, close to micromanagement; little individualisation, support or positive feedback; instruction given as "how I would do it" instead of a positive sequence.
  • Expertise ≠ leadership (L1): her technical excellence is not translating into leadership. McClelland: high need for achievement, doing it herself.
  • Motivation (SDT): seniors' autonomy is frustrated, so they disengage; newcomers' competence need is unmet because there is no feedback. Relatedness is weakened. Seniors may also carry resentment from the promotion (an LMX "out-group" risk).
  • Situation: the director's results-only view pushes a results-oriented process ("if it works don't fix it") and ignores human outcomes.
  • Partly an adaptive challenge: Marta must change her own assumptions about what her job is.

Alternative interpretations: the drop could also come from market factors. Check pipeline data before concluding it is all leadership (avoid confirmation bias).

Antecedents: technical favourability high (competent team); psychological favourability low (seniors' motivation, newcomers' confidence); situational favourability mixed (pressure from the director).

Interventions:

  • Relational: 1:1s with the two seniors to listen (active listening, validate + redirect), recognise their expertise and give each ownership of a key account or a mentoring role (individualisation, decentralised active management). Fortnightly 1:1s with newcomers using the positive instruction sequence and SBI feedback.
  • Contextual: replace "Marta reviews everything" with clear quality criteria, peer review in pairs (senior + newcomer), and Marta reviewing only proposals above a threshold (responsible delegation with an escalation rule). Weekly team meeting with Keep · Drop · Add.
  • Hierarchical: Marta negotiates with the director a short-term target plus a few human indicators for the next quarter (vision and instruction upwards to improve situational favourability).
  • Marta herself: rewrite her philosophy, practice and criteria so they are congruent; a mentor or coach helps her "go to the balcony".

Criteria: % of proposals sent without Marta's rewrite (target ≥ 70% by month 2); 1:1s held (≥ 90%); seniors' participation in meetings (observation); pulse items on autonomy and "I know how I'm doing" (≥ 4/5 at month 3); retention of the senior; sales recovered to the previous level within two quarters.

Practice case B · The merged warehouse

Case

After a merger, two logistics teams (12 people from the original company, 8 from the acquired one) now share one warehouse under a new supervisor, João. Each group keeps its own procedures, sits on its own side of the warehouse and takes breaks separately. Errors in shipments have doubled. In meetings the original team dominates the discussion; the acquired team stays quiet and later complains among themselves. João has offered a bonus to the team with fewer errors each month. The situation got worse.

Model answer (try it first)

Problems: a group, not a team (no interdependence or shared goal); a faultline along company of origin; stuck in storming with conflict going underground; diversity without inclusion (the acquired team is present but not heard, so tokenism); low psychological safety for the minority; two procedures, which is a contextual problem.

Why the bonus failed: it is external regulation and a transactional exchange that reinforces the faultline: it rewards competition between the two subgroups instead of a shared goal. It treats an adaptive challenge (integration, trust) as a technical one (incentive).

Interventions: hierarchical: one single procedure as a non-negotiable goal, with a clear rationale; replace the competing bonus with a shared team goal. Contextual: mixed shifts and mixed pairs; one shared break area; process design by a mixed working group (decentralised active management); meeting rules: silent input first, rotating facilitation, leader speaks last. Relational: 1:1s with members of the acquired team; validate their concerns; individualise roles to use their expertise. Behaviours: vision ("one warehouse, zero errors"), support, positive feedback on cooperation, instruction on the new procedure.

Criteria: shipment error rate back to pre-merger level within 3 months; % of shifts with mixed pairs; speaking time or participation of both groups in meetings (observer count); psychological safety item with no gap between groups; single procedure documented by week 6.

The Leadership Plan

35% of the grade · submission 30/11 · built from the sheets completed at the end of each lecture

Confirmed in class: the plan is written in the second half of the class on 30 November, using the sheets you completed after each lecture (returned next week). Structure below follows the course's own model: the Pro●Leader leadership plan (Gomes & Morais, 2025, Figure 5), built on the LEM.

What the plan is (and is not)

In Pro●Leader the whole programme is "centred on the formulation of a leadership plan, which is individualised for each participant". Participants:

  1. choose a real scenario in their lives where they can influence others (a team at work, a student association, a sports team, a project group, a family business);
  2. define their leadership cycle: philosophy, practice, criteria (conceptual cycle);
  3. adjust it to the antecedent factors: their own characteristics, their followers', the situation;
  4. select the leadership behaviours needed to implement it (optimal profile);
  5. train it through role-play (leader, followers, observers), which is the practical cycle.

The plan is not an essay about theories. It is a personal, concrete and evaluable design, where theory justifies each choice.

Section by section: how to write it well

Before Section 1: describe the scenario

In Pro●Leader's role-play, the leader first describes the context, the followers and the main purpose of the plan. Do the same: who you lead (number, profiles, maturity), where (organisation, constraints, your power), what for (the challenge), and for how long.

Section 1 · Philosophy

  • One short mission sentence + 2–4 core values/principles. Positive, specific, personal, ethical (Gomes, 2020).
  • Show where it comes from: your leadership story, values, the theories that convince you (e.g. "I believe people perform better when they feel autonomous and competent: SDT").
  • Answer "is it important to my team?": connect it to what they need.
  • Use the lenses if helpful: what leading means to you (ontology), values (axiology), ethical limits (ethics), how you live it (phenomenology).

Section 2 · Practice

  • Every value in Section 1 must have at least one concrete, observable action. No orphan values.
  • Write actions as behaviours with frequency: "Fortnightly 30-minute 1:1 with each member", not "I will be close to my team".
  • Cover the five tasks: establish the mission, unite the team, define the plan, apply it, define outcomes.
  • Use the three levels of intervention (relational, contextual, hierarchical).
  • Include how you'll communicate, give feedback (SBI + A), handle conflict, delegate.

Section 3 · Criteria

  • For every practice: an indicator that is aligned, clear, specific, measurable, with source and review date.
  • Mix implementation (did I do it?) and consequences (task and human).
  • Mix subjective and objective efficacy.
  • Say what you will do if a criterion is not met: the feedback loop.

Section 4 · Challenges (antecedent factors)

  • You: strengths and risks (personality, bias you're prone to, preferred conflict style, Leadership Orientations results) and how you compensate.
  • Members: technical and psychological maturity, motives (McClelland), diversity, possible faultlines.
  • Situation: power, resources, deadlines, culture, what your organisation expects.
  • For each: facilitator or inhibitor? And the adjustment you make to the plan.

Section 5 · Styles and behaviours

  • Tick the behaviours you use and say where in the cycle and why (use the five-tasks table in 1.6).
  • Aim for the optimal profile: all five transformational behaviours, positive feedback, decentralised active management where maturity allows.
  • Negative feedback: say when (only breaches of agreed standards) and what you'll use instead first (corrective instruction).
  • Passive management: say how you'll avoid it.

Quality checklist

A strong plan…
  • Has a specific scenario (who, where, what for, how long)
  • States a short, positive, ethical, personal philosophy
  • Traces each value → action → indicator (no orphans)
  • Uses observable, dated actions at the three intervention levels
  • Has criteria with source and review date, for implementation and consequences, task and human
  • Analyses leader, members and situation, and shows the adjustment
  • Justifies each chosen behaviour with the optimal profile
  • Answers "is it important to my team?" in every section
  • Includes a feedback loop: what you'll do when reality differs
  • Names concepts precisely (e.g. "decentralised active management", not "being democratic")
Common mistakes
  • Philosophy as a list of buzzwords ("trust, innovation, excellence") with no meaning
  • Practice that describes intentions ("I'll be supportive"), not behaviours
  • Criteria only about results (sales, grades)
  • Criteria that cannot be measured ("people are happy")
  • Ignoring followers: plan written only from the leader's view
  • Treating negative feedback or centralised control as the main tool
  • Confusing Bass's MBE-active with LEM's active management
  • No mention of your own weaknesses or biases
  • Theory summaries instead of application

Theories in 30 seconds

Communication & conflict
